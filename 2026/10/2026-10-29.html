Anthropic

Company
"How AI agents shape experts' judgment calls": analysts adopted the agent's coin-flipped preference 86% of the time
SIGNIFICANT
Oct 27, 2026 (missed yesterday) · Anthropic Research

Design. 61 professional analysts took part: 50 used Claude and 11 declined. All had passed causal-inference knowledge checks, and of the AI users, 65% had a master's, a doctorate or 6+ years of experience. Each had to advise a fictional farming cooperative on expanding an advisory program, making four analytical decisions with two defensible options each, then a recommendation. A secret coin flip set which option Claude's agent favored on each decision.

Findings.

  • Analysts picked the agent's option 86% of the time: 92% on outcome measurement, 82% on comparison method, 81% on farm coverage and 90% on weighting.
  • Analysts who had stated a preference switched 60% of the time when the agent disagreed.
  • 77% used the agent's arguments verbatim, and only 15% credited it. 52% still rated the recommendation as more their own than the agent's.
  • The benefit: analysts without AI made serious errors 55% of the time, against 6% with it.

Anthropic: "If experts defer to agents without realizing it, the agents' preferences—which could be arbitrary or misaligned—could shape important decisions." Context: this connects to the Oct 16 finding that agents wait on humans 80% of the time. The bottleneck is shifting from the work to the human's judgment, and this study suggests that judgment is itself being steered.

arXiv: LLMs grading olympiad proofs; why group-decision memory fails
Oct 27, 2026 · 2610.36767 · 2610.37624
"Can LLMs Reliably Grade Olympiad Proofs?" (Ospanov et al.) tests 20 models on graded USAMO 2025 solutions and finds "considerable room for improvement." Its MABGrader method, which uses multi-armed bandits, improves Quadratic Weighted Kappa by 26.4% and cuts grading error by 14 points. That matters given this month's AI-proof claims. "Rare, Relevant, and Still Wrong" introduces CrossProfile. Relevance-based note selection loses 23–31 points to rarest-first, which itself does no better than random. Models also overweight facts attributed to many agents, and removing attribution tags adds 13–24 points. Full transcripts did worse than no notes at all.

Notable Themes

Frontier releases now reach defenders first

Gemini 4 Carbon follows the pattern Argon set: top benchmark scores, but available only to Fairwind cyber defenders. Mythos and Devin Security were also gated by cyber capability. The lead features are now cyber scores (SEC-bench Pro, ExploitGym) rather than chat benchmarks. A week after php2shell, Loukides's "The PHP Incident" explains why: the labs see exploitation capability as the thing to stage carefully.

The human in the loop is getting steered

Anthropic's 86% deference result, Tornhill's "human attention doesn't" scale, and DAYJOB: Tax's finding that judgment is the bottleneck all point the same way. Agents now make the moment-to-moment choices, people adopt them without noticing (77% reused the agent's arguments verbatim, but only 15% credited it), and the hard part, professional judgment, is where agents still fail. LangChain's 70% scheduled runs show more of the work starting without a person at all.

Benchmarks are tightening and splitting

AA v5 moved to hidden sets and new domains. Lambert argues it now measures the frontier rather than open models' economic niche, and Carbon took the top spot within hours. The olympiad-grading paper shows LLM verifiers are still unreliable. DAYJOB: Tax's 95% pass bar puts every model near 10%. Which benchmark you pick now largely decides who wins.

Safety refusals can be used as a weapon

The nullpt.rs script turns model refusals into obfuscation: closed models wouldn't touch it and an open one would. The same split appeared in the Hugging Face breach, per HN, and in Cohere's Qwen findings last week. Closed-model safeguards now affect defenders as well as attackers, and that pushes defenders toward open weights.
